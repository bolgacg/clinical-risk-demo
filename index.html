Section 2 · The console

One patient, one screen

Real data: the NIDDK Pima diabetes cohort, 724 patients after excluding known missing-value codes, a public proxy for the registry data the group actually works with. Three things to know:

  1. Pick a patient, or build one. The chips load four real patients from the cohort; every slider is a measurement you can change, and the model answers on every movement.
  2. The model explains itself. The bars show how much each measurement pushes this patient's risk up or down, and the button computes the smallest clinically actionable change that would move the patient out of the referral band.
  3. Sometimes it refuses. When a patient sits outside the data the model was trained on, the console withholds the number and says why. That state is the thesis of this page.

The actionable question

What is the smallest change, in measurements a clinic can actually influence, that moves this patient below the referral threshold?

The console refuses to counterfactual age or family history: an explanation a clinician cannot act on is not advice. Glucose, blood pressure and body mass index are the movable levers here.

Terms used on this page
risk · the model's estimated probability that this patient develops the condition, from an ensemble of thirty models
attribution · how much each measurement pushes this patient's risk up or down, relative to the cohort average
ensemble disagreement · thirty models trained on resamplings of the same data; when they stop agreeing, the estimate is not stable
outside the data · this patient's measurements sit far from everything the model was trained on, so no output deserves trust
calibration · when the model says 40%, the condition should occur in about 40% of such patients; the study below measures this
counterfactual · the smallest change to a patient's actionable measurements that changes the model's recommendation
referral threshold · the risk level above which a patient is flagged for follow-up; a clinical capacity decision, not a mathematical one

Limitations

The data is the public NIDDK Pima cohort, a proxy chosen so the page can be fully reproducible; the group's registry data is richer and harder, and the point here is the design discipline, not the dataset. The model is a bootstrap ensemble of logistic regressions: deliberately simple, so every attribution is exact rather than approximated. Rows with known missing-value codes are excluded and the exclusion is stated. Nothing on this page is medical advice, and no clinical claim is made beyond what a 724-patient public cohort can support.
